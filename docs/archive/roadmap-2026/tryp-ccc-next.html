Chapter 00

Where we actually are

Worth writing down, because half of the ideas people suggest for a community like this are already built and the other half assume something that is not.

Built and running

  • One network, six markets nested inside it (UK & Ireland, Spain, Portugal, Germany, Romania, Nordics)
  • Challenges with briefs, entries, leaderboards, prizes and a participation voucher
  • Per-market rooms, worldwide rooms, DMs with a request model
  • Connections, creator directory, live creator map, travel collab board
  • Daily games, resource library, events, referrals, invoicing and payouts
  • A points engine, wired but deliberately not switched on everywhere

The honest gaps

  • There is no identity ladder. Everybody is “a creator” on day one and on day four hundred.
  • Rewards are cash prizes for winning. Nothing rewards showing up for a year.
  • The community produces video. It does not yet produce reviews, photography, local knowledge, or product feedback — all of which Tryp.com needs and none of which we ask for.
  • We measure views. We do not measure what a creator is worth to the business, so we cannot argue for budget.
  • Nothing physical has ever arrived in a creator’s hands.
The through-line for everything below. The programme currently pays for output. The next version should pay for belonging, because belonging is what produces output for years instead of for one campaign, and it is dramatically cheaper per unit of content.
Decision 01

How far do we gamify?

You asked whether to add points, leaderboards and the rest of it, or whether the views leaderboard is enough. This is the most consequential decision in the document, because it is very hard to reverse.

What the evidence says

The overjustification effect

Attaching points to something people already enjoy makes them dependent on the points, and less interested once the points stop. Creators post travel content because they like making travel content. Paying them in abstract currency for it is the one intervention that can reliably make them like it less.

Global leaderboards demotivate

A single ranked list of everybody motivates the top five and discourages everyone else. With 44 creators we would be publishing a weekly reminder to 39 people that they are not winning.

Badge inflation

Badges work when they are scarce and genuinely hard. A badge anybody can get for logging in signals that logging in is what we value. We already have effort-based badges on profiles; adding twenty more would devalue the ones that mean something.

Recommendation: do not build a points economy. Keep the views leaderboard, per challenge — it is not really gamification, it is the scoreboard of a competition with real money attached, and that is a different thing. Then add recognition in three forms that do not have the failure modes above: seasonal (resets, so everyone starts level), segmented (most improved, best first video, most helpful in the rooms — many winners, not one), and milestone (a ladder you climb by persistence rather than by beating anyone).

What that means concretely

BuildDo not build
Per-challenge views leaderboard (already live) A single all-time platform leaderboard
A season: 3 months, resets, its own table and its own small prize Lifetime points that only ever go up
Milestones: 5 videos, 25 videos, one year, first million views XP, levels, streaks, daily login rewards
Peer-nominated awards decided monthly by the community Automated badges for trivial actions
Private progress (“you are 2 videos from your voucher”) Public ranking of who posts least

The points engine already in the database stays. It is per-challenge and opt-in per brief, which is exactly the shape that survives this recommendation: a challenge can choose to be scored on points without the platform becoming a points game.

Decision 02

What we call people

You floated “Tryp.com Ambassador”, and a Campus Leaders or City Lead programme inside it. Both are right, and they are answering two different questions that should not share one name.

Identity: what you ARE

Earned by time and contribution. Everybody has one. It never expires and it is what goes in a creator’s Instagram bio. This is the ladder.

Role: what you DO

A job, with responsibilities and usually a budget or a stipend. Few people have one. It can be handed back. This is the org chart.

The identity ladder

NameHow you get thereWhat it unlocks
Tryp.com CreatorAccepted into the programmeBriefs, rooms, the map, challenges
Tryp.com Ambassador5 published videos or 3 months activeMerch drop, profile badge, early briefs, name on the site
Tryp.com Senior Ambassador25 videos or a year inTravel voucher, first refusal on paid work, a vote on next quarter’s briefs
Tryp.com InsiderInvitation only, ~10 people worldwideProduct roadmap access, an annual trip, direct line to the team

Four rungs, not seven. Every rung has to be worth putting in a bio, and the gap between rungs has to be a year of real work, or the top one means nothing.

The role structure

RoleWhat it is
Tryp.com CCC Lead
Content Creator Community Lead		 You. One person, cannot be removed by anyone else, can hand it on. Built and live.
Country Manager
e.g. “Spain Country Manager”		 Runs one market: its briefs, its rooms, its recruitment, its budget. Paid or stipended. Titles are live now on /admin/team.
City Lead
e.g. “Lisbon City Lead”		 Volunteer. Runs meetups in one city, welcomes new creators there, knows the good spots. Rewarded in vouchers and status, not salary. This is the Airbnb Host Club model and it is the single best-proven structure in this space.
Campus Lead A student who runs Tryp.com at one university. Deliberately a separate programme, not a rung — see below.
On Campus Leaders. Keep it separate from the creator ladder and give it its own name and its own front door. A campus programme is an acquisition channel: the goal is bookings from students, and the people who are good at it are organisers rather than videographers. Folding it into the creator ladder would blur what “Ambassador” means and put us in the position of ranking a great organiser below a mediocre filmmaker. Run it as Tryp.com on Campus, let people hold both, and let the two feed each other.

Decision 03

What the community is for, beyond content

Right now the community exists to produce videos for challenges. That is one use of 44 engaged travellers who love the brand, and it is not the most valuable one.

Eight things this community can do that a media buy cannot

1. Paid social creative

Whitelisted creator video run as ads from the creator’s own handle typically beats studio-produced brand ads on cost per order. We are already producing the assets and using them once.

2. Destination content at scale

Every landing page for every city needs photography, a paragraph, and three things to do. We have people standing in those cities.

3. Reviews and trust

The single highest-converting asset in travel is other travellers. Creators can seed genuine, disclosed reviews of real trips.

4. Product feedback

44 people who book flights constantly and will tell you exactly where the funnel hurts, for free, if asked properly.

5. Local intelligence

Which routes are cheap right now, what is on in Porto in October, where the airport transfer is a trap. This is merchandising input.

6. Recruitment

Creators recruit creators far better than ads do. The referral flow exists; the reward is too small to move anyone.

7. Events and presence

A Tryp.com meetup in six cities is brand presence at a cost a media plan cannot approach.

8. Distribution for launches

A new market, a new feature, a sale: 44 accounts posting inside 48 hours is a launch channel we own.

Recommendation. Add exactly one non-video ask per quarter, and pay for it properly. Start with destination content (highest commercial value, easiest to brief, most reusable). Do not add all eight at once: a community asked for eight different things becomes a community that does none of them.
Chapter 04

Milestones, rewards and merch

A ladder of things to reach for, costed. The principle throughout: status and access are cheaper than cash and are remembered longer, so cash should be reserved for the moments where nothing else will do.

The milestone ladder

MilestoneRewardWhy this reward
First video publishedWelcome pack: stickers, luggage tag, handwritten noteThe cheapest loyalty you will ever buy. Physical post is disproportionately memorable.
3 videosProfile badge + featured in the newsletterFree. Recognition, at the point where people decide whether this is for them.
5 videos / 3 monthsAmbassador status + the merch dropThe rung that has to feel like arriving. Merch is the visible proof.
10 videos£50 travel voucherSpendable on the product, so the cost is marginal rather than face value.
25 videos / 1 yearSenior Ambassador + £150 voucher + first refusal on paid workAccess is worth more than the voucher and costs nothing.
1M cumulative viewsFlight to a Tryp.com trip, or a £300 voucherThe headline milestone. Should be rare, public and photographed.
2 years activeInsider invitationTime served, not output. Rewards the quiet reliable people the leaderboard never sees.
Brought in 3 creators£100 voucher + “Recruiter” note on profileCurrent referral reward is too small to change behaviour.

Merch: what, and when

Tier 1 — Welcome

Sticker sheet, luggage tag, card. ~£3. Everyone who publishes once.

Tier 2 — Ambassador

Heavyweight tee or cap, packing cube, enamel pin. ~£25. Earned, never sold.

Tier 3 — Senior / Insider

Numbered jacket or holdall with the creator’s name. ~£70. Fewer than 15 a year.

Three rules that decide whether merch works

  • Never sell it. The moment it is purchasable it stops being a marker of status and becomes a t-shirt. A shop is a different business; this is a badge.
  • Make it good enough to wear when not filming. Cheap merch is worse than no merch: it tells people what they are worth. One excellent item beats five printed ones.
  • Number and name it. “Ambassador 014” embroidered inside the collar costs almost nothing and converts a garment into an artefact.

Practical note: merch needs an address, a size and a delivery status per creator. That is a small feature (nos. 20–23 below) and it should ship before the first order, not after.

Chapter 06

What we should refuse to build

A roadmap without this section is a wish list. Each of these is a reasonable idea that would make the product worse.

A universal points economy

Covered in chapter 01. It would make the intrinsically motivated creators — the good ones — care less.

An all-time global leaderboard

Motivates five people and quietly tells 39 they are losing.

Streaks and daily login rewards

This is not a language app. Punishing somebody for being on a flight is absurd.

A merch shop

The moment it is purchasable it stops being status. Sell merch and you have a small clothing business and no badge.

An in-app video editor

Creators have CapCut and Premiere and are better at them than we will ever be.

Auto-generated content

The whole asset class we sell is “a real person went there”.

Public follower counts

Turns a community of peers into a hierarchy in one deploy, and the briefs do not care.

Ranking creators publicly by earnings

The fastest way to make people who like each other stop.

Chapter 07

The next 90 days

Everything marked NOW above, sequenced so each month ends with something a creator would actually notice.

Month 1 — Say what people are

  • Ship the identity ladder and backfill everybody into it (29)
  • Milestone engine plus private progress (15, 16)
  • Shipping details, sizes and the fulfilment queue (20, 21)
  • Order the welcome pack and the Ambassador drop
  • Community guidelines, written and agreed at signup (52)

Month 2 — Make the rooms worth opening

  • Unread counts, pinned welcomes, the weekly prompt (42, 45, 48)
  • Brief templates, scheduled challenges, reference reels (1, 2, 7)
  • Deadline reminders to non-entrants only (9)
  • The playbook restructure and the hook bank (73, 74)
  • First welcome packs land in the post

Month 3 — Prove what it is worth

  • Cost per view and attributed bookings, properly tracked (93, 94)
  • Usage rights captured up front, then the first whitelisted ads (96, 103)
  • First paid destination-content commissions (104)
  • Market health dashboard and the launch checklist (83, 84)
  • Creator NPS, asked and answered in public (100)
The one number to watch. Not views, and not entries. The share of creators who published something this month. A programme where 80% of people post is worth more than one where 20% post twice as much, because the first one compounds and the second one is four freelancers and a mailing list.
