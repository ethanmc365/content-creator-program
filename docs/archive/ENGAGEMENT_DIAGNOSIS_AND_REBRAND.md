# Why nobody is engaging, and what to do about it

3 August 2026. Every number here is measured against production, not estimated.

---

## Part 1: The diagnosis

### Start with the number that reframes the question

**22 of your 43 approved creators have never opened the app. Not once.**
`last_seen_at` is null for exactly half the community.

That single fact changes what you are looking at. You have been asking "why is
engagement low" as though you have 43 people who are here and quiet. You do not.
You have roughly 21 people who have ever arrived, and of those, a smaller group
who do anything. The engagement problem is mostly an **arrival** problem, and
arrival problems have completely different fixes to motivation problems.

### Four things are actually going on

**1. There is no prompt, so there is no behaviour.**

The standard model here is Fogg's: a behaviour happens when motivation, ability
and a **prompt** arrive at the same moment. You can have a community of highly
motivated creators and get nothing if nothing ever taps them on the shoulder.

7% push adoption means that when a challenge goes live, 40 of 43 people find out
only if they independently decide to open a web app they have no habit of
opening. That is not an engagement funnel, it is a lottery. And until this
morning, general chat created no notification at all, so even the people *with*
push heard nothing when the room was active.

This is the cheapest and largest lever you have, and it is not a motivation
question at all.

**2. The room is empty, and empty rooms stay empty.**

31 messages exist in total, and two thirds are yours. Anyone who opens #general
sees a room where the only voice is the organiser's. Social proof runs in both
directions: visible activity invites participation, and visible silence forbids
it. Posting into a dead channel carries real social risk (nobody replies, in
front of everyone) for no reward.

Note what *did* work: **50 connections and 206 game plays**. Both are private,
low-risk, single-player-ish actions where you cannot be embarrassed. People are
willing to engage — they are not willing to be the first person to speak.

**3. A tournament demotivates almost everyone in it.**

This is the part worth being blunt about, because it is a design choice rather
than an accident.

Your live challenge has a £190 pot across 3 winners, with 11 entrants. Expected
value for an entrant is about **£17**, and for a creator looking at the
leaderboard the realistic expectation is lower still, because they can see the
top creator on 13,400 views and themselves on a few hundred. Expectancy theory
says effort collapses when someone believes effort will not change the outcome —
and for the bottom two thirds of any leaderboard, that belief is correct. They
are behaving rationally.

Worse, there is a second-order effect. Framing content creation as a paid contest
can **crowd out** the intrinsic reasons people make travel content in the first
place — craft, identity, growing their own audience. This is the overjustification
effect, and it is well replicated. Once creating is "the thing you do to try to
win £63", the people who do not expect to win have been given a reason *not* to
create, where before they had no reason either way.

So you have an activity with weak extrinsic pull (small, unlikely reward) that
has also partly displaced the intrinsic pull. That is the worst of both.

**4. The brief is open, and open briefs suppress output.**

The current challenge is "create any video featuring Tryp.com". Open briefs feel
generous and are experienced as work. A creator has to decide the concept, the
angle, the hook and the format before they can start, and every one of those
decisions is a place to stall for 13 days. Constraint is what makes creative
tasks easy to start. "Film the view from your window and say where you'd rather
be" gets made tonight; "make any video" gets made never.

### What this means in order of leverage

1. **Reachability.** Get push adoption from 7% to 60%+. Nothing else you do
   matters as much, and none of it is a design problem.
2. **Activation.** 22 people have never arrived. They need a reason to come once
   and something worth finding when they do.
3. **Make the room look alive** before asking anyone to speak in it.
4. **Then** fix the motivation model, which is Part 2.

## Part 2: Rebranding to ambassadors

You asked the right question: *if we drop the competition, why would anyone
create content?*

The answer is that the competition was never really why they created. Only 11
people have ever entered, and the top few would almost certainly have made
travel content anyway. What the contest actually provides is a **deadline and a
reason to point their camera at Tryp.com**. You can supply both without a
tournament, and get better economics doing it.

### The problem with "ambassador" as usually done

Most ambassador programmes fail in the opposite direction to contests. They
hand out a title and a discount code, ask for posts in return, and discover that
a title with nothing behind it motivates nobody for more than a fortnight. If
you rebrand and do only that, engagement will get worse, not better.

An ambassador model works when three things are true, and all three are things
a contest cannot give you.

### The three things that make it work

**1. Certainty replaces the lottery.**

Every accepted post earns something known in advance. Not a chance of £63 — a
certain £10, or £15, or a travel credit. People will do meaningfully more work
for a small certain reward than a large unlikely one, because the mental maths
actually resolves. Your current pot of £190 across 3 winners could instead be
19 guaranteed £10 payments, which at your current numbers would reward *every
single post that has ever been made* and still cost the same.

This also fixes your CPM. Guaranteed-rate posts mean spend scales with output
rather than being fixed regardless of it.

**2. Status is front-loaded, not competed for.**

In a contest, status is the prize and 90% of people don't get it. In an
ambassador programme, status is the *entry condition* — you were selected, you
are one of 40 Tryp.com ambassadors — and then it is maintained rather than won.
That is a much stronger motivational structure, because identity ("I'm a Tryp
ambassador") drives behaviour far more durably than a possible future payout.
People act consistently with the identity they have accepted.

Make the selection feel real: a cap on numbers, a visible badge, an announcement
when someone joins, a real bar to entry. Scarcity at the door, not at the prize.

**3. The reciprocity runs the other way first.**

The contest model asks creators to give first (make a video) and *maybe* receive.
Flip it. Give first: trip credit, early access to Tryp.com features, their
content reposted to Tryp's own channels, a genuine say in the product, a real
travel discount they'd use anyway. Then the ask lands on someone who has already
received something, and reciprocity does the work that prize money was failing to
do.

The most valuable currency you have is not cash — it is **audience and access**.
Reposting a creator's video to Tryp.com's channels is free to you and worth more
to a growing creator than £63.

### What the model looks like concretely

**Name and frame.** "Tryp.com Ambassadors" or "The Tryp Collective". Members
are ambassadors, not entrants. The programme has a roster, not a leaderboard.

**Tiers, earned by contribution, not by ranking against each other:**

| Tier | How you get there | What it gives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ambassador | Accepted into the programme | Badge, £X per accepted post, community access |
| Featured | 3 accepted posts | Higher rate, content reposted by Tryp.com, profile on the site |
| Core | Sustained contribution over a quarter | Trip credit, early access, input on product, first refusal on paid briefs |

Everyone can reach every tier. Nobody is competing with anybody. This is the
single most important structural difference and it is what fixes the expectancy
collapse.

**Briefs replace challenges.** Short, specific, frequent, optional. "This week:
your best 15 seconds of a place nobody's heard of." A brief is a prompt with a
deadline, which is what the contest was actually providing.

**Keep exactly one competitive thing.** A monthly "best of" with a real prize,
sitting on top of the guaranteed-rate base. Contests are excellent at generating
peak effort from the people already at the top; they are terrible as the only
mechanism. Use it as a garnish rather than the meal.

### What you would lose

Be clear-eyed: guaranteed rates cost more per post than a fixed pot when volume
goes up. That is the point — you would be paying for output instead of paying
regardless of output — but it means the budget stops being predictable. Cap it
per month and say so.

You also lose the leaderboard drama that makes the top 3 creators push hard. The
monthly "best of" is there to keep that.

### How to test it cheaply

Do not rebrand everything at once. Run the next challenge as a guaranteed-rate
brief with the same total budget: "every accepted post earns £10, up to 19
posts." Compare posts, unique creators, and CPM against the £190/3-winners
challenge you have just run. You now have the analytics to measure exactly that,
and one month of data will tell you more than any argument about it will.

---

## The three things to do first

1. **Ask for push notifications during onboarding**, not buried in Settings.
   7% to 60% adoption changes everything downstream, and it is a day of work.
2. **Seed the room before inviting people into it.** A weekly prompt in
   #general that expects a one-line answer, and a personal DM to the 22 people
   who have never logged in, will do more than any feature.
3. **Run one guaranteed-rate brief** against the same budget and compare the
   numbers. Decide the rebrand on evidence you will have in a month.
